HOW THE OPTIMIZATION WAS DONE
Zirconia oxygen sensor: predicting O2 concentration (C) from pressure (P) and current (I)
Data: zirconia_sensor_complete_experimental_data.xlsx (21 % to 95 % O2)
=====================================================================================

THE GOAL IN ONE LINE
--------------------
We have a physics equation that turns P and I into C. When we put the real
measurements in, it does not give exactly 21 %, 30 % ... 95 %.
So we "tune" a few numbers until its answers are as close as possible to the
known concentrations.

This is done in three steps:
  Step 0: outlier screen. Leave out readings that clearly disagree with all the others.
  Stage 1: physics only. Tune A and B inside the physics equation.
  Stage 2: correction. Fix the leftover error by adjusting the result
           (adding to it and multiplying it).


THE DATA
--------
27 pressures x 14 known concentrations
(21, 30, 35, 40, 45, 50, 55, 60, 65, 70, 75, 80, 85, 95 %) = 378 readings.
Each reading = (pressure, current, true concentration).
There is no 90 % column in the sheet; empty columns are skipped automatically.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STEP 0: OUTLIER SCREEN (which readings to trust)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Some readings do not agree with the rest of the data at all:
  - The whole "60 %" column has LOWER currents than the 55 % column at every
    pressure. Its currents behave like about 49 % O2, so it is probably a
    mislabelled or mis-transcribed column.
  - The first three 95 % readings (P = 56.89, 62.65, 65.71: 59.8, 59.9, 67.5 uA)
    are far below the rest of that column, which then jumps to 115 uA.

How the computer finds them (no hand-picking):
  1. Fit the whole model (Stage 1 + Stage 2) on the readings currently trusted.
  2. Measure every reading's error.
  3. Compute a "robust spread" of the errors: 1.4826 x median absolute deviation
     (MAD). Unlike a normal standard deviation, a few huge errors cannot inflate it.
  4. Leave out any reading whose error is more than 3.5 x that spread from the median.
  5. Repeat until the set of left-out readings stops changing.

Result: 30 of 378 readings left out (all 27 at "60 %" and 3 at 95 %).
The other 348 readings are used. The excluded points are still shown in the UI
as hollow grey markers. You can switch the screen off in Parameters
(Exclude outliers = 0) or change the 3.5 cut-off.

Worth checking in the original notebook as well (kept in the fit, but suspicious):
  - 85 % column: every value from P = 144.2 onwards is about 30 uA higher
    (99.61 -> 129.79), a sudden step.
  - 80 % column: a similar step around P = 360 (102.6 -> 112.6 -> 120.9).


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STAGE 1: PHYSICS ONLY (finding A and B)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The equation
  C = 100 x [ 1 - exp( -(I R T L / 4 F S P) x (A/Dk + B/Db) ) ]

  Dk = Knudsen diffusion (oxygen hitting the pore walls). It does not depend on pressure.
  Db = bulk diffusion (oxygen hitting other gas molecules). It gets smaller as pressure rises.
  A  = how strongly the Knudsen part counts
  B  = how strongly the bulk part counts
  When A = 1 and B = 1 we have the pure textbook theory.

What "best A and B" means
  For every trusted reading:
      error = predicted C - true C
  Square each error (so minus and plus errors both count, and big errors
  count a lot more), then add them all up:
      Loss(A, B) = sum of (error)^2
  The best A and B are the pair that make this Loss as small as possible.

How the computer finds them (simple version)
  Step 1. A quick first guess.
          The equation can be rearranged as
              -ln(1 - C/100) = A x p + B x q
          where p and q are numbers we can compute from P, I and the physics
          constants. This is a straight-line problem in A and B, so ordinary
          least squares (the usual "line of best fit") gives a first guess at once:
          A = 1.312, B = 0.846.

  Step 2. Improve the guess step by step (Levenberg-Marquardt method).
          Think of the Loss as a landscape of hills and valleys, where every
          point (A, B) has a height equal to the Loss. We want the lowest point.
            - Stand at the current (A, B).
            - Look at how the errors change if A or B is nudged a little
              (this is the "slope", called the Jacobian).
            - Take a step downhill.
            - If the step made the Loss smaller, keep it and take bolder steps.
              If it made the Loss bigger, undo it and take a smaller, more careful step.
            - Repeat until the Loss stops getting smaller.
          We start from the theory point (A = 1, B = 1) and also from the
          Step 1 guess, then keep whichever ends up lower.
          A and B are searched as log(A) and log(B) so they can never go negative.

  Step 3. Check that A and B are trustworthy.
          From the slopes at the best point we compute the uncertainty (+/-) of A and B
          and how strongly they depend on each other (correlation).
          The correlation is -0.80, which is below 0.95. That means the data can tell A and B apart.
          This works because at low pressure the Knudsen part dominates (57 % of the
          resistance) and at high pressure the bulk part dominates (90 %).

Result of Stage 1
  A = 1.028 (+/- 0.024)     B = 0.949 (+/- 0.010)
  Took 5 improvement steps. Loss went from 2757 down to 2437.
  Average error (RMSE): 2.82 % (theory A=B=1)  ->  2.65 % (optimized A, B)

  With the new data, the physics law already works well from about 40 % to 75 % and at 95 %
  (average errors of 0.4 to 1.9 %).

Why Stage 1 is not enough
  The physics says that at a fixed pressure the current follows -ln(1 - C/100).
  The real sensor does not follow that exact shape at every concentration:
  the 21 % current is lower than the law expects (about 5 % too low after the fit),
  and 30-35 % come out too high. A and B can only stretch the whole curve up or down
  at each pressure. They cannot bend its shape. No values of A and B can fix this.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STAGE 2: CORRECTION (adjusting the result)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Idea
  Keep A and B from Stage 1, and keep the physics answer (call it Cp).
  Then pass Cp through a small correction formula that adds to it and multiplies it:

      C_final = k0 + k1 x Cp + k2 x Cp^2 + k3 x Cp^3

  (The earlier 4-level data used k0 + k1 x exp(k2 x Cp). That shape cannot follow
  the 21-95 % range, so it was replaced by this cubic.)

How the k values are found
  This formula is a straight-line problem in k0..k3, so ordinary least squares
  solves it exactly in one step (no iterations needed).
  Safety checks:
    - The curve must only go UP across the calibrated band (Cp 14.4 to 95.8 %), so a
      higher current always gives a higher concentration. If the cubic failed
      this check, the code would fall back to a quadratic, then a straight line.
    - Outside that band the curve continues as a straight line (it never turns back),
      and the answer is kept between 0 and 100 %.
  Other shapes were also tried (correction in log space, pressure-dependent terms).
  They were either worse (2.3-3.0 %) or only slightly better with more parameters
  (1.7 %), so the simple cubic was kept.

Result of Stage 2
  k0 = 19.281   k1 = -0.2945   k2 = 0.02500   k3 = -0.0001448
  Average error (RMSE): 2.65 %  ->  1.90 %      R^2: 0.985 -> 0.992
  Example: P = 56.89, I = 8.082 (true value 21 %)
      theory 19.09 %   physics only 18.93 %   physics + correction 21.68 %

Where did this formula come from? (honest answer)
  It is NOT derived from physics. It is an empirical calibration curve: several shapes
  were tried on the data and this one gave the lowest error on unseen pressures.
  The physics part (A, B) stays physical; the cubic only fixes the leftover shape.

Why is k0 = 19? Is 19 % being added to every reading?
  No. k0 is the value of the curve at Cp = 0. The data only covers Cp = 14.4 to 95.8 %,
  so Cp = 0 is far outside it, and the code never uses the cubic there (below 14.4 % it
  continues as a straight line). With powers of Cp up to 95^3 = 857,000, the four k values
  balance each other, so each one alone means little.
  Written around the middle of the data (Cp = 55.1 %), the SAME curve is:

      C = 54.73 + 1.142 (Cp - 55.1) + 0.00107 (Cp - 55.1)^2 - 0.000145 (Cp - 55.1)^3

  So at Cp = 55.1 % it gives 54.7 % with slope 1.14: close to "C = Cp" (no change),
  plus a small S-shaped bend. That bend is the whole correction.

Why not a LINEAR correction C = k0 + k1 x Cp?
  We tested it. The best straight line is  C = 0.30 + 0.996 x Cp,  which is almost
  exactly "C = Cp". Error: 2.647 % -> 2.644 % (no real change).
  Reason: A and B already do every straight-line adjustment that helps. Least squares
  makes the errors average out to zero and removes any overall tilt, so a straight
  line has nothing left to fix. Only a CURVED correction can reduce the error further,
  because the remaining problem is the shape (21 % too low, 30-35 % too high,
  75-85 % off), not an offset or a scale.
  (There is also no "exponential of an exponential" in the current formula. The older
  k0 + k1 exp(k2 Cp) form, used for the 21-40 % data, did put an exp on top of the
  physics exp; it was replaced because it could not follow 21-95 %.)


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
WHICH PARTS ARE WORTH OPTIMIZING? (all variants on the same 348 readings)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  "Unseen" = leave-one-pressure-out. "Correct level" = unseen prediction rounded to the
  nearest concentration level.

  Model                    params   A       B       RMSE    unseen   correct level
  Theory (A = B = 1)          0     1       1       2.815   2.815    70.7 %
  Only A (B = 1)              1     0.9334  1       2.737   2.742    74.1 %
  A and B                     2     1.0278  0.9494  2.647   2.649    80.2 %
  Only A + linear             3     0.9334  1       2.721   2.745    76.4 %
  A, B + linear               4     1.0278  0.9494  2.644   2.663    78.7 %
  Only A + cubic              5     0.9334  1       1.968   1.998    91.4 %
  A, B + cubic (default)      6     1.0278  0.9494  1.903   1.928    91.1 %

  What this shows:
  - Optimizing only A (B fixed at 1) already beats theory a little: 2.82 -> 2.74 %.
    Adding B takes it to 2.65 %.
  - A linear correction adds nothing (see above).
  - The cubic correction gives the big gain (about 2.7 -> 2.0 %), with or without B.
  You can choose any of these in the UI (Parameters -> "Stage 1" and "Stage 2"), or in
  model.py with DEFAULT_CONFIG "fit_B" (1 = A and B, 0 = only A) and
  "correction_degree" (0 = none, 1 = linear, 3 = cubic).

  Only A is optimized like this:
    -ln(1 - C/100) = A p + 1 x q   ->   first guess  A = sum(p (X - q)) / sum(p^2)
    then Levenberg-Marquardt over log A only (code: fit_A_only in model.py).

  The outlier screen always uses the A, B + cubic model, so every option is scored
  on the same 348 readings. (If each model screened itself, a weak model would throw
  away the readings it cannot fit and look better than it really is.)


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
DID WE JUST MEMORIZE THE DATA? (validation)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Test 1: Hide every 4th pressure, re-tune everything on the rest, then
          predict the hidden ones.     Error on hidden data: 1.89 %
  Test 2: Hide one pressure at a time (27 times), re-tune, predict it.
          Error: 1.93 %  (almost the same as 1.90 % on all data)
  So the model works on readings it has never seen. It is not just memorizing.

  Summary (all in % O2, on the 348 trusted readings)
                          RMSE    hidden-pressure RMSE
    Theory A=B=1          2.82    -
    Physics (A, B)        2.65    2.65
    Physics + correction  1.90    1.93


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
WHY NOT EXACTLY 0 % ERROR?
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Some measurements go against the trend. For example, the 21 % current drops from
  9.80 to 8.90 uA when pressure rises from 87.72 to 92.18, and the 85 % and 80 % columns
  have sudden steps. The workbook notes also say several handwritten digits are hard
  to read. The largest remaining errors are at 85 % (about 3.6 %) and 95 % (about 2.4 %; at 95 % the
  physics-only model is actually better, about 1.0 %, because the cubic flattens near the top).
  Fixing the transcriptions in the sheet and re-running is the best way to improve.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
USING THE MODEL (prediction)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Only P and I are needed:
    1. Compute Dk and Db for this pressure and temperature.
    2. Cp      = physics equation with A = 1.028, B = 0.949
    3. C_final = 19.281 - 0.2945 Cp + 0.02500 Cp^2 - 0.0001448 Cp^3
  Trust it inside the tested range: P 56.89 to 595, C 21 to 95 %.
  The UI shows a warning when a reading is outside that range.


NOTE ON PARAMETERS
  The sensor geometry (pore radius, area, thickness) and the temperature
  (700 C) are placeholders. If you change them, A and B change to
  compensate, but the predicted concentrations stay the same. Enter the real
  sensor values if you want A and B to have a physical meaning.
  The pressure unit is assumed to be kPa (not stated in the sheet).

RETRAINING ON NEW DATA
  Put the new .xlsx in this folder with the same layout (first column Pressure,
  then one column per concentration with "NN%" in the header) and restart the app.
  It reads zirconia_sensor_complete_experimental_data.xlsx by default; otherwise the
  newest .xlsx in the folder (or set the ZIRCONIA_DATA environment variable).

Code: model.py -> find_outliers() (Step 0), fit_AB() or fit_A_only() (Stage 1),
      fit_correction() (Stage 2), levenberg_marquardt() (the step-by-step minimizer).
